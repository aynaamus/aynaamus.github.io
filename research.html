Introduction

What Six Investigations Add Up To

This collection brings together six investigations into changes in the global art market and the structures that developed around them. The papers examine the expansion of Asian buying power, the movement of high-value artworks through public and private transactions, the development and limitations of restitution law, the relationship between political and family histories and the commercial art market, changes in the ownership and financing of major auction houses, and the growth of private collecting and private museums. Together, they trace a period in which changes in the geography of the market coincided with changes in art ownership and in the institutions responsible for its circulation.

One of the most significant developments has been the movement of buying power toward Asia. Japan played an early role in this shift as corporate and private wealth entered the Western auction market at increasingly high price levels. After the contraction of the Japanese market, buyers from Hong Kong, Taiwan, and mainland China became increasingly important participants. China’s economic expansion created a new class of collectors, while domestic auction houses developed alongside Sotheby’s and Christie’s. Hong Kong became a major point of connection between Chinese capital and the international market, eventually establishing itself as one of the principal centers of the global art trade.

The growth of Asian buying power occurred alongside changes in the market’s infrastructure. Auction houses expanded their operations in China and Hong Kong, while Chinese auction businesses developed rapidly within the domestic market. Different regulatory and ownership arrangements shaped how international houses could operate in mainland China, while Hong Kong provided a more established location for international transactions. The result was a market increasingly distributed across several major centers rather than organized primarily around New York and London.

The movement of artworks within this expanded market is more difficult to follow than public auction results alone indicate. Public auctions represent only one part of the trade. A substantial share of transactions takes place through dealers, galleries, auction-house private sales, and direct transactions between collectors. These exchanges often produce far less public information about price, buyer identity, or subsequent ownership. A work that appears publicly at auction may therefore leave the public record after its sale and remain outside documented exhibition or resale for long periods.

Private transactions can also involve ownership structures that make the movement of individual works more difficult to reconstruct. The person associated publicly with a purchase may not necessarily be the person who financed it, while companies and offshore entities can appear between the artwork and its ultimate owner. These ownership structures limit what can be established from public sales records alone.

Competing legal claims expose the limits of ownership records and restitution mechanisms. The international system of cultural-property restitution developed primarily in response to conquest, wartime seizure, forced sales, organized looting, and the large-scale displacement of cultural objects. Over time, the Hague Conventions, UNESCO, UNIDROIT, national legislation, and international agreements established principles and procedures intended to identify unlawfully transferred objects and return them to earlier owners or their heirs.

These mechanisms depend on identifiable works, documented ownership, a known possessor, and an authority capable of enforcing a claim. Different national legal systems impose different restrictions, while many international agreements remain non-binding or apply only to particular periods and forms of transfer. Restitution law therefore operates within limits created not only by law itself but also by incomplete ownership records, jurisdiction, the passage of time, and the difficulty of locating privately held works.

The development of the Chinese auction market included direct overlaps between commercial positions, ownership stakes, financial interests, family relationships, and the political history of modern China. Senior positions and major ownership interests within the auction market intersected with families connected to China’s twentieth-century political leadership. These relationships form part of the history of the Chinese art market’s expansion.

The collection also examines changes in the companies responsible for facilitating the international trade in art. Sotheby’s and Christie’s developed under different ownership structures that subjected them to different levels of shareholder scrutiny and financial disclosure and affected their use of guarantees, private sales, and commercial risk. Sotheby’s return to private ownership ended the public disclosure requirements associated with its stock-market listing and changed the financial structure under which the company operated. That transition occurred while the company was simultaneously increasing its commitment to Asia and expanding its presence in China and Hong Kong.

Private collecting represents another major part of this expansion. As Chinese wealth entered the international art market, collectors acquired both Chinese and Western works at increasingly high levels. Some collections remained private, while others became the basis for new museums and exhibition spaces. Private collecting provided the basis for the creation of the Long Museum and the public exhibition of works from the collection.

The later return of works from major private collections to auction also demonstrated that accumulation and resale form different stages of the same market. Collections assembled during long periods of rising prices eventually encounter different market conditions when works are offered again. Their resale returns privately held works to public auction and provides evidence of how earlier acquisitions perform under changed market conditions.

Chapter One: The Eastward Shift

How Asian Capital Remade the Global Art Market

On the evening of November 9, 2022, sixty paintings went under the hammer at Christie’s Rockefeller Center saleroom in New York and, in under three hours, brought in $1.5 billion – the largest total ever recorded for a single auction.1 All sixty lots sold. A Georges Seurat fetched $149.2 million. A Paul Cézanne shattered the artist’s own auction record at $137.8 million. A Gustav Klimt more than doubled the $40 million its owner had paid for it sixteen years earlier. A Van Gogh brought $117.1 million, a Gauguin $105.7 million. Five paintings broke the $100 million mark in a single evening – something that had never happened before in the history of the market.2

“Visionary: The Paul G. Allen Collection Part I” was sold, per the late Microsoft co-founder’s instructions, entirely for philanthropy. At the time, it read as confirmation that the art market’s long boom still had room to run. Eighteen months later, it looks more like the top of a cycle. Rising interest rates and tightening art-backed credit cooled the market through 2023; Christie’s global sales fell by double digits year over year, and a speculative bubble in young artists’ work lost roughly a third of its value.3

The Allen sale is a useful hinge for a longer story, because it landed at the precise moment two multi-decade trends crossed: the Western auction market reaching the top of a financialized, globalized cycle it had been riding since the 1980s, and the buying power behind that market completing a forty-year migration east. To understand why the market cooled the way it did after 2022 – and why so much of what happens next depends on Beijing, Shanghai and Hong Kong rather than New York and London – it helps to start with a rehearsal that took place three decades earlier, in Tokyo.

A rehearsal in Tokyo

In 1987, at a London auction, Yasuda Fire and Marine Insurance paid a then-record $39.9 million for Vincent van Gogh’s Sunflowers – a purchase timed, deliberately, to mark the firm’s hundredth anniversary. It made the front page around the world, and it set off a buying spree among Japanese corporations flush with cash from an overheated economy and a rapidly inflating asset bubble. By 1990, near the top of that bubble, Japan was importing more than $4 billion in art annually and had absorbed nearly half of all Impressionist paintings that came to market that year. Ryoei Saito, chairman of the Daishowa Paper Manufacturing empire, spent a combined $160 million at a single sale on a Van Gogh and a Renoir – at the time, the two most expensive paintings ever sold.4

Four years later, the bubble burst. Japan’s banking system seized up, corporate balance sheets buckled, and the same firms that had bought trophy Impressionist paintings as inflation hedges and prestige objects became forced sellers, unloading masterpieces at a fraction of what they had paid. Some of that art disappeared into bankruptcy proceedings and has never resurfaced. What mattered for the market’s longer trajectory, though, was who was buying on the way down: a small but growing cohort of collectors from Hong Kong, Taiwan and, increasingly, mainland China, purchasing undervalued Western paintings just as Japanese demand withdrew.5 It was a minor episode in 1993. In retrospect, it was the opening move in a much longer game – the first time Asian capital stepped into a vacuum left by a Western downturn. It would not be the last.

Building the infrastructure

China’s own art market did not emerge from nowhere; it was built, deliberately, over two decades. The country’s economic growth through the 1990s and 2000s created a new class of wealthy investors who initially parked their money in real estate. As Beijing gradually loosened the rules around art transactions, that capital found a second outlet.

China Guardian, the country’s first major auction house, was founded in Beijing on May 17, 1993, by Chen Dongsheng. Its first sale, held March 27, 1994, brought in RMB 14.23 million, then roughly $1.6 million, according to Chinese-language company records; within a decade it had become one of the largest auction houses in the world by turnover.6 In 2005, Beijing Poly International Auction launched as a subsidiary of China Poly Group, a roughly $40 billion state-owned conglomerate with roots in the military-industrial sector. Backed by state capital and protected market access, Poly grew fast enough to become, by most measures, the world’s third-largest auction house within a decade of its founding – behind only Christie’s and Sotheby’s.7

Tax policy took longer to catch up, and moved in both directions before it helped. Before 2000, China’s customs schedule did not treat art as its own category at all; in 2007, tax authorities formally added cultural relics and artworks to the import tariff schedule for the first time, grouping them with luxury goods and setting the rate at 12 percent – a real cost that discouraged bringing foreign works onto the mainland and encouraged collectors to keep transactions offshore.8 Only from 2012 did that reverse, with the tariff cut to 6 percent and, for some categories, down to 3 percent by 2017 – a friendlier regime, but one that arrived after, not before, Sotheby’s and Christie’s had already made their opening moves into China.

The arrival of the two Western majors followed a telling asymmetry. Sotheby’s entered mainland China first, in September 2012, through a joint venture with Beijing Gehua Art Company, a state-owned enterprise: Sotheby’s held 80 percent of the new entity and operational control, but its Chinese partner was, by definition, an arm of the state.9 Christie’s took a different and slower path, becoming in 2013 the first international auction house licensed to operate independently in China under its own name – a genuine wholly foreign-owned enterprise, with no state joint-venture partner, though barred from trading cultural relics predating 1911.10 The difference was not merely structural. A foreign house operating without a state partner invited more scrutiny of what, exactly, it was moving in and out of the country; it is one reason serious Chinese buyers of high-value Western art have often preferred to transact through Hong Kong or offshore vehicles rather than directly on the mainland, and why Hong Kong – not Beijing or Shanghai – became the real seat of China’s international art trade.

The turn

Return, now, to that November evening in 2022. Even as the Allen sale set records, the conditions that would end the cycle were already forming. Interest rates were rising sharply after a decade near zero; inflation was running hot; lenders were pulling back from the art-backed financing that had let dealers and speculators hold inventory on credit. Collectors who might otherwise have sold began instead to hold their best works rather than test a softening market – and in an asset class as psychologically driven as fine art, reduced supply from top holders can chill demand for everything beneath it.

Nowhere was the reversal sharper than in the market for very young artists. During the pandemic, buying a painting practically “wet” from the studio and flipping it at auction within months – once a practice serious collectors avoided, for fear of being blamed for destabilizing an artist’s market – became almost routine, as art turned into a short-term speculative asset for a new class of buyers. The numbers tell the story: auction sales of work by “ultra-contemporary” artists (industry shorthand for those born in 1975 or later) rose more than sevenfold in under a decade, from $91.4 million in 2013 to $739.3 million in 2021, with the three major houses alone generating over $570 million from the category that year.11 By 2023, prices for the same cohort of artists had fallen by close to a third from their 2021 peak, as speculative buyers who had counted on quick resale discovered that both enthusiasm and financing could evaporate at the same time.

A market turning inward, and a generation turning over

The cooling wasn’t only about price; it changed who was buying, and what. By 2022, Chinese dealers had shifted overwhelmingly toward local buyers: roughly 76 percent of sales nationally went to collectors within China, with the mainland figure closer to 87 percent against 50 percent in Hong Kong, where the market’s older international reach still brought in meaningful overseas participation. Japan showed a similar pivot, with roughly 81 percent of art bought there going to Japanese collectors.12

Taste shifted alongside geography. Old Masters had a bad year across the board – a 17 percent revenue decline that pushed the category back to roughly $963 million, close to 2020 levels, driven substantially by cancelled sales in China13 – while modern and contemporary art continued gaining ground, capturing close to a third of the Chinese market and pulling share away from the two categories that had defined Chinese collecting for a generation: calligraphy, which fell 11 percent in revenue yet still held nearly half the market at 47 percent, and ceramics, whose revenue fell more sharply, around 31 percent, on a 28 percent market share.14

That shift had a face, or several. A cohort of ultra-contemporary Chinese artists – Kao Yu, Sun Hao and Yan Bing among them – saw unusually strong bidding in 2022 from a younger generation of buyers close to their own age. Sun Hao’s Wan Shui Qian Shan (“Ten Thousand Rivers and Mountains”), a 2019 mirror-mounted work measuring 280 by 800 centimeters, set his auction record at China Guardian’s spring 2022 sale, hammering at RMB 7.2 million against a presale estimate of RMB 3–5 million and selling with premium for RMB 8.28 million, or roughly $1.2 million, leading the house’s Chinese contemporary painting and calligraphy session15 – a specific, checkable data point for what was, more broadly, a generational handoff: the same collectors who had spent a decade chasing Impressionist trophies and Ming porcelain were being joined, and in places replaced, by buyers in their twenties and thirties collecting artists born in their own lifetime.

Hong Kong’s ceiling

China’s own market absorbed a harder shock at the aggregate level. Pandemic-era disruption cut the country’s total art-auction turnover by roughly a third in 2022 – from about $5.9 billion to $3.9 billion16 – even as, paradoxically, Hong Kong was consolidating its position as the world’s second-largest marketplace for contemporary art, behind New York and ahead of London, for a second consecutive year. In 2022 alone, Christie’s, Sotheby’s and Phillips together hammered more than $1 billion worth of art in Hong Kong.17 Worth noting for scale: China’s market had actually proven more resilient than the rest of the world at the start of the pandemic, falling only about 15 percent in 2020 against a roughly 20 percent global decline, before the harder 2022 contraction described above.

That rise in Hong Kong’s standing, though, came with a ceiling at the very top of the market. Industry reporting on the 2022 season pointed to a retreat among the highest-value results specifically: only five Hong Kong results made the global top 100 auction sales in 2022, down sharply from 22 the year before, even as the city’s overall standing as a marketplace improved.18

Who owns the room

However the numbers around any single year shake out, the broader structure of the market is not in dispute: it is extraordinarily concentrated, and concentrated in a strikingly similar way across very different national markets. In 2022, the United States, China and the United Kingdom together accounted for 76 percent of global public auction sales by value, with the U.S. alone taking 37 percent.19 Look inside any one of those countries and the pattern repeats at the level of individual firms: the ten largest auction houses reportedly account for more than 70 percent of revenue in the United States, close to 60 percent in both China and the UK, and 54 percent in France.20 At the level of the two biggest global names specifically, Sotheby’s and Christie’s together represent something on the order of 40 to 45 percent of global fine-art auction turnover in an ordinary year; in 2023, Sotheby’s edged into the lead with roughly 26 percent of global turnover to Christie’s 24 percent, as the two majors together saw combined sales fall by about 13 percent from the prior year.21

That decline was not evenly distributed, and it ran in different directions depending on where a house sat relative to China’s own recovery. Christie’s, whose spectacular 2022 numbers had been inflated by the one-off Paul Allen estate, saw its total sales fall from $8.4 billion in 2022 to $6.2 billion in 2023 – a drop of roughly a quarter on a headline basis, and a much smaller one (about 12 percent) once the Allen sale is stripped out of the comparison.22 Bonhams, by contrast, had the best year in its 230-year history in 2023, with turnover above $1.2 billion, up 14 percent from 2022.23 Chinese houses specifically were reported at the time to be staging a sharp recovery from a weak 2022: Poly International and Xiling Yinshe each posted triple-digit percentage gains, on the order of 170 and 180 percent respectively.24 China’s top mainland houses were recovering share even as Christie’s contracted, part of a broader pattern in which the market’s center of gravity continued shifting toward Asia even as its Western anchors struggled.

The single-work results from 2023 tell a similar geographic story. The year’s highest price was Pablo Picasso’s Woman with a Watch, which sold at Sotheby’s New York for $139.3 million, the second-highest price ever paid for a Picasso; Claude Monet’s Le bassin aux nymphéas followed at $74 million at Christie’s New York, having stayed inside the same family’s collection for more than fifty years before the sale.25 Gustav Klimt’s Dame mit Fächer set a record for the artist and became the most valuable work of art ever sold at a European auction when it went for $108.4 million in London, alongside a second Klimt, Island in the Attersee, at $53.2 million in New York, and Jean-Michel Basquiat’s El Gran Espectáculo (The Nile) at $46 million.26 Hong Kong’s contribution to that same year showed up less in headline single-lot prices than in overall revenue share: both Christie’s and Sotheby’s reported that Asia, and Hong Kong specifically, accounted for roughly 16 to 19 percent of their global revenue in 2023.27

A pattern with a history

None of this should read as a single, isolated shock. The Western auction establishment has weathered a remarkably similar sequence of crises before, and Asian capital has, on each occasion, played a role in absorbing it. The early-1990s recession, compounded by the bursting of Japan’s asset bubble, forced a wave of high-end reselling at depressed prices – precisely the opening that early Hong Kong, Taiwan and mainland Chinese buyers seized. Later in that decade, Sotheby’s and Christie’s were caught colluding on the commissions they charged sellers, a six-year conspiracy that overcharged clients by roughly $400 million combined; Sotheby’s chairman, A. Alfred Taubman, was sentenced to a year and a day in prison and fined $7.5 million, with the trial judge finding he had been “the initiating and driving force” behind the scheme.28 The 2008 financial crisis hit the market again, and it hit the guarantee system the houses had built during the boom years directly: Sotheby’s alone had committed $902 million in guarantees to consignors in 2007, and by the end of 2008 the two majors together were sitting on roughly $63 million in losses from guarantees gone bad, forcing Sotheby’s to cut its outstanding guarantee book from $458.5 million to $306.1 million within months and triggering the layoffs that follow almost automatically when an auction house’s balance sheet suddenly looks like a hedge fund’s.29 Sotheby’s own stock fell 83 percent in the crash.30 Once again, the retrenchment coincided with a newly wealthy Chinese investor class gaining confidence at exactly the moment Western buying power contracted, and both major houses responded by expanding their presence in Hong Kong. The COVID-19 pandemic forced a sudden shift to online sales in 2020, and it was demand from Asia, more than from New York or London, that kept results respectable through the worst of it. The 2023 slowdown added a new and more overtly geopolitical wrinkle to the same pattern: alongside the usual pressures of high interest rates and inflation, the market lost a meaningful slice of its highest-spending participants when sanctioned Russian oligarchs, cut off from Western financial and auction infrastructure, simply stopped showing up.

The pattern is real, but it is worth stating plainly rather than triumphantly: each Western downturn has coincided with the emergence, or re-emergence, of a wealthier and more confident buying class somewhere in Asia – first Japan, then Hong Kong and Taiwan, then mainland China – with enough dry powder and enough appetite for prestige assets to keep the top of the market liquid when Western demand faltered. It is a structural feature of the last forty years of art-market history, not a coincidence confined to any single cycle.

What the Allen sale actually marked

It is tempting, in hindsight, to read the November 2022 sale as the last blowout of an old order – the final night the Western auction establishment could produce a number that large on the strength of Western demand alone. That is too tidy. Asian bidders, private and institutional, were active participants in the room that night, part of a buyer base that had already become genuinely global. What the sale actually marked was not an ending so much as a changing of the terms: the same forces that produced a $1.5 billion evening – cheap credit, a decade of runaway prices, a growing pool of buyers using art as a store of value rather than an object of scholarship – were also the forces that had, over the preceding decade, been quietly moving an unknown but very large number of the world’s most important paintings out of public auction rooms entirely and into private hands, many of them in Asia, few of them likely to reappear soon.

Chapter Two: The Vanishing Masterpiece

What Happens to Great Art After It Leaves the Auction Room

On November 4, 2014, a Van Gogh still life, Vase with Daisies and Poppies – painted in June 1890, its flowers gathered from the field where the artist would attempt suicide weeks later – sold at Sotheby’s in New York for a hammer price of $55 million, $61.765 million with fees.31 Huayi Brothers, one of China’s biggest film studios, confirmed to Chinese media that the buyer was its chairman, Wang Zhongjun, acting in a personal capacity rather than on the company’s behalf.32 For a while, that was the whole story: a movie mogul had bought a van Gogh.

It was not the whole story. According to a New York Times investigation published in May 2023, corporate records show the purchase was actually financed by Xiao Jianhua, a reclusive Chinese financier who ran a hidden offshore empire of more than 130 companies through Hong Kong, with the money for the painting routed through an intermediary and a British Virgin Islands shell company. In January 2017, Xiao was taken from his Hong Kong apartment by mainland Chinese agents – a rendition so brazen it made headlines worldwide – and was later sentenced to 13 years in prison for market manipulation and bribery. The painting has not been publicly exhibited since. Former Sotheby’s staff and a New York art adviser told the Times it had at one point been quietly shopped for private sale, at a proposed value near $70 million. Nobody outside a small circle of dealers appears to know who owns it now, or where it is.33

“Nobody needs a $62 million van Gogh, and nobody wants to buy a lawsuit,” the art lawyer Thomas C. Danziger told the Times34 – meaning that once a painting’s ownership becomes even faintly complicated, serious buyers simply walk away, and the work effectively drops out of circulation. That single sentence describes something larger than one painting. A meaningful share of the most valuable art in the world is not lost, stolen, or destroyed. It is simply no longer visible – sold once, quietly, and then gone from the record.

The part of the market you can’t see

It is tempting to treat “disappearing art” as scandal or conspiracy. Mostly, it is structure. In 2025, the global art market moved an estimated $59.6 billion. Of that, public auctions – the sales that make headlines, get catalogued, and leave a price on the record – accounted for roughly 35 percent. Private sales brokered directly through the auction houses added another 7 percent, and these are almost never disclosed: no price, often no buyer’s name, sometimes not even confirmation that a sale happened at all. The largest slice of the market, some 58 percent, moves through dealers and galleries, a world that keeps records for its own purposes and essentially none for the public’s.35 The art critic Georgina Adam devoted an entire book to this structural opacity, documenting how private sales, asset swaps between collectors, and offshore holding arrangements have let major works change hands, sometimes never leaving climate-controlled storage, without a price or a buyer’s name ever reaching the public record.36

Put plainly: roughly two-thirds of everything that changes hands in the art world happens off the public record by design, not by accident. Auctions are the visible tip of a much larger and permanently invisible transaction volume. That baseline matters for everything that follows, because it means the question is not really “why does art disappear?” The honest question is closer to “why are we ever able to see it at all?” – and the answer is that a public auction is essentially an opt-in event. Once a work sells there once, at a price the seller and buyer were both willing to have printed in the trade press, nothing obliges anyone to ever do that again.

A partial census: Van Gogh

Because most of the market is invisible by default, it is hard to measure “disappearance” in aggregate with any rigor – a problem this piece returns to below. But it is possible to look closely at one artist and count.

Van Gogh is a useful test case because his highest-value works trade rarely and are tracked closely by the trade press. Of his seven most expensive paintings ever sold at auction, at least three – and by some counts more – have gone to Chinese buyers since 2014: the $62 million still life bought by Wang Zhongjun (with Xiao Jianhua’s financing, per the Times); The Avenue of Les Alyscamps, sold for $66 million in 2015 to a Taiwanese gallerist believed to be bidding on behalf of an unnamed mainland collector; and Labourer in a Field, sold for $81 million in 2017 to an anonymous Chinese buyer whose identity has never been confirmed publicly. A fourth major work, Young Man with a Cornflower, drew heavy East Asian bidding interest when it sold in 2021.37 Like the $62 million still life, none of the other three appears in any public exhibition catalogue since its sale – though that is based on the absence of a record, which is a weaker thing to assert than a confirmed fact, and is precisely the kind of claim this piece is arguing you should be able to check and mostly can’t. Since 2000, “new emerging markets for Van Gogh’s work have opened up, particularly among Russians, Koreans and, above all, the Chinese”38 – and a disproportionate number of the resulting sales have gone quiet immediately afterward.

This is not unique to Van Gogh, and it is not unique to Chinese buyers – European and American private collectors have removed plenty of masterpieces from view over the past century, and institutions do it too. What is comparatively new is the scale and speed: paintings that once might have stayed in a single Western family’s hands, occasionally lent to museums, for two or three generations before reappearing at auction now sometimes vanish within a single sale.

Getting the story right: two paintings, not one

One correction is worth making explicitly, because it is easy to conflate two different paintings that share a market moment. Claude Monet’s Meules – a grainstack painting from his famous 1890–91 series – sold at Sotheby’s in May 2019 for $110.7 million, a record for the artist and the first Impressionist work ever to clear $100 million at auction. Sotheby’s has never disclosed the buyer, and the painting has not surfaced publicly since.39 It is frequently, and incorrectly, misattributed to Van Gogh in secondhand accounts of the sale – an understandable mix-up, since it sits in the same $100-million-plus bracket, the same Impressionist-and-Modern sale category, and the same pattern of anonymous, subsequently invisible ownership as the Van Gogh works described above. But it is a Monet, sold in 2019, not a Van Gogh sold in 2017, and getting the attribution right matters, because conflating the two makes the underlying pattern look more mysterious than it needs to: this is not one mistakenly-labeled disappearance, but two separate, well-documented ones.

A useful counter-example, because it complicates any simple story about “Asian money erasing art from public view,” is Amedeo Modigliani’s Nu Couché. Liu Yiqian, the Shanghai billionaire, bought it at Christie’s in November 2015 for $170.4 million after a nine-minute bidding war among seven bidders – at the time the second-highest price ever paid for a work of art at auction.40 Liu and his wife, Wang Wei, bought it explicitly for their Long Museum in Shanghai, where, unlike the Van Gogh and the Monet, it has been on public view. The difference is not the buyer’s nationality or wealth; both the Modigliani and the vanished Van Gogh went to Chinese collectors at comparable price points in the same decade. The difference is intent. Some of this money is buying trophies for private vaults. Some of it is building museums.

Where the big numbers come from – and where they don’t

Discussions of this subject tend to reach for large, precise-sounding aggregate figures: that Asian buyers have acquired 60,000 to 70,000 Western artworks since 1980; that they accounted for 30 to 40 percent of high-value Western art purchases between 2008 and 2023; that 20 to 50 percent of high-end acquisitions happen off-market; that up to half of all masterpieces over $10 million have become untraceable. These numbers circulate widely in art-market commentary, are frequently attributed in general terms to “Sotheby’s and Christie’s” or unnamed “insiders,” and are almost never traceable to a specific report, dataset, or named methodology.

None of these figures trace to a specific report, dataset, or named methodology. That does not mean they are wrong – a great deal of real activity in this market is, again, structurally invisible, and no comprehensive public dataset of private art transactions exists for anyone to check such a claim against. But it means they are best treated as informed estimates circulating within the trade rather than as measured facts. The difficulty of sourcing even the headline statistics about the market’s opacity is itself a small demonstration of the underlying problem: a market this hard to see is also a market that is hard to accurately describe, and that includes the people describing it.

What can be said with confidence is narrower and arguably more useful: a specific, named, checkable set of paintings by one of the world’s most-tracked artists has gone quiet after sale to Asian buyers over the past decade; the broader market structure makes that kind of disappearance the norm rather than the exception for any high-value private transaction; and at least one well-documented case shows that the identity of a painting’s public buyer and the identity of the person who actually paid for it can be two entirely different – and, in that instance, criminally consequential – things.

When museums start selling

The private market’s opacity is one half of this story. The other is a live, public debate about whether the institutions built specifically to keep art visible should start behaving more like the private market – selling from their own collections under financial pressure.

This is not a hypothetical. In April 2020, as pandemic closures gutted museum revenue, the Association of Art Museum Directors – the body whose rules govern deaccessioning at nearly every major U.S. museum – suspended its own long-standing restriction that sale proceeds could only fund new acquisitions, for a two-year window allowing proceeds to cover the “direct care” of existing collections, which in practice meant some institutions used it to help cover payroll.41 The Baltimore Museum of Art tested the limits of that window a few months later, announcing plans to raise $65 million for staff pay raises, diversity initiatives, and free admission by selling three works: Andy Warhol’s The Last Supper (1986), privately, for a projected $40 million; Brice Marden’s 3 (1987–88), estimated at $10–15 million; and Clyfford Still’s 1957-G (1957), estimated at $12–18 million. The backlash – board resignations, an open letter from museum leaders nationally, sustained public criticism – forced the museum’s trustees to call off the sale hours before the Still and Marden were due on the block.42 Two years later, in October 2022, the AAMD went further than the temporary pandemic exception and permanently loosened its rule, formally allowing proceeds from deaccessioned works to fund the “direct care” of a museum’s remaining collection on an ongoing basis – not unanimously: 109 of 199 member directors voted in favor, 21 against.43

The Baltimore fight was, in miniature, an argument about exactly the same question the private market answers by default every day: is a great painting a public trust, or a financial asset that happens to hang on a wall? Private collectors mostly settled that question in the market’s favor years ago. What makes the museum world’s version of the debate worth watching is that, unlike a private sale, it happens in public, with named institutions, recorded votes, and a paper trail – which is precisely what makes it possible to write about with any confidence, in sharp contrast to almost everything else in this piece.

Chapter Three: Objects of Restitution

Two Centuries of Trying to Give Looted Art Back

In 1815, a convoy of wagons left Paris carrying several thousand paintings and sculptures back toward Italy, Germany, Austria and the Netherlands. Napoleon had spent two decades systematically stripping the museums, churches and palaces of conquered Europe and installing the best of it in the Louvre – not incidentally, but as policy, a deliberate statement that Paris was now the capital of Western civilization. When the allied powers who defeated him gathered at the Congress of Vienna in 1814–15 to redraw the continent’s borders, they also did something that had no real precedent in European diplomacy: they decided that some of what Napoleon had taken should go back. It is, as far as the historical record shows, the first time a group of nations formally agreed that military conquest did not carry with it a right to another country’s art.

It did not go very far. A great deal of what Napoleon looted stayed in French hands, quietly, because there was no mechanism to force the issue beyond the victors’ goodwill and, in some cases, their armies literally standing in Paris. That pattern – a war forces a grudging admission that looted culture should be returned, followed by a settlement too weak, too narrow, or too late to make it happen consistently – repeats for the next two hundred years. This piece traces that pattern from Vienna to the present, because the law built out of it is, in theory, the machinery that is supposed to resolve exactly this kind of ownership dispute: who actually gets to keep a given painting, and what happens when the answer is contested.

That machinery matters here for a specific reason. From Vienna onward, it was built to handle one kind of disappearance: art seized by force, by an identifiable actor, in a conflict with a knowable end and a knowable victor able to compel its return. It has almost nothing to say about the kind of disappearance this collection is otherwise concerned with: a painting that vanishes not because it was stolen but because it was bought, then folded into a shell company or a family holding structure no wartime statute was ever written to see through. That gap is the argument of this chapter. The two centuries of legal history that follow are worth reading in full, because the law’s specific, repeated failures (what counts as retroactive, what counts as binding, who counts as a claimant) are what eventually produce the gap. But it helps to know where the history is headed before it starts: toward a legal apparatus extremely well built for one kind of theft, and structurally blind to the kind that has largely replaced it.

A slow start, 1814–1919

For most of the nineteenth century, the Vienna precedent was more gesture than law. In 1874, European governments met in Brussels to try to write clearer rules of war, motivated partly by the destructiveness of the Franco-Prussian War; a draft declaration explicitly stated that seizing cultural property was illegal, but no government ever ratified it. It nonetheless shaped the thinking behind the next real attempt: the Hague Peace Conferences of 1899 and 1907, which produced the first legally binding international treaties addressing the protection of cultural property in wartime.44 Crucially, the Hague rules were not retroactive – anything already looted before 1899 or 1907 simply stayed where it was, a design choice that would recur in nearly every restitution instrument that followed, right up to the present day.

The Hague rules were essentially ignored during the First World War, particularly by German forces occupying Belgium and northern France, where museums and libraries were looted and damaged extensively. The Treaty of Versailles in 1919 built restitution obligations into the postwar settlement and required the return of some stolen material – a real if narrow precedent that legal claims to looted art could be enforced as part of a peace settlement, not just hoped for.

The great theft, 1930–1945

The next chapter is different in scale and in kind, because it starts with an ideology rather than a war. It is also the best-documented episode in this history, owing largely to Lynn Nicholas’s The Rape of Europa, the definitive account of Nazi art plunder and the wartime and postwar scramble to reverse it.45 In 1930, the Nazi theorist Alfred Rosenberg published The Myth of the Twentieth Century, a tract that framed modern art – abstraction, emotional distortion, anything that broke from academic realism – as evidence of racial and cultural degeneration. When Hitler took power in 1933, Rosenberg’s framework became policy. By 1937 the regime had opened the Haus der Deutschen Kunst in Munich to showcase state-approved art, while a parallel “Degenerate Art” exhibition toured German cities to ridicule everything else. In Berlin in 1939, the regime destroyed roughly 4,000 works confiscated under this program – an act of ideological theater as much as art disposal.46

Ideology very quickly became a cover for theft. After the 1938 annexation of Austria, Nazi authorities began systematically seizing art from Jewish collectors – the Rothschilds, the Bloch-Bauers, and many less famous families – under the pretext of “safeguarding” it. Senior Nazi officials treated this as a personal opportunity as much as a state project: Hermann Göring built one of the era’s great private collections, including works by Picasso, Matisse and Braque that his own regime had publicly condemned as degenerate. The looting was formalized in October 1940 with the creation of the Einsatzstab Reichsleiter Rosenberg, a task force that catalogued and shipped art out of every territory the Wehrmacht occupied, sorting it into what the Reich wanted to keep, what it planned to use as a bargaining chip in a future peace, and what it considered worthless “degenerate” material – some of which the ERR itself destroyed by fire at the Jeu de Paume museum in Paris in July 1943, rather than let it circulate.47 A French museum curator named Rose Valland, working undercover at the Jeu de Paume while the Nazis used it as a sorting depot, secretly recorded the destination of thousands of stolen works – intelligence that would prove essential to Allied recovery efforts after the war.48

The invasion of the Soviet Union in June 1941 opened a second, even larger front. German forces looted the Hermitage and the Pushkin Museum and, by the most-cited postwar tallies, damaged or destroyed several hundred Soviet museums and well over one hundred million books, while a separate postwar Soviet catalogue lists more than 1.1 million individual museum items as stolen or lost – on top of stripping and dismantling the Catherine Palace’s famed Amber Room, which has never been definitively recovered.49 Soviet-era statistics on the exact scale of this destruction vary by source and were subject to decades of political revision, but the broad picture – hundreds of museums and libraries destroyed or emptied – is not seriously disputed.

An imperfect peace, 1945–1955

As Allied forces advanced in 1944 and 1945, specialist units – the “Monuments Men,” officially the Monuments, Fine Arts and Archives program – located Nazi hoards hidden in salt mines, castles and rail depots and began the enormous task of sorting and returning them.50 What to do with the recovered material became its own postwar argument. At a Moscow conference on reparations in June 1945, and again at Potsdam in July, the Allies disagreed: Britain resisted treating art as war reparations at all, while the Soviet Union in particular treated it as exactly that. No unified policy emerged; each occupying power went its own way.

In the American zone, General Lucius Clay devised a three-part framework: art stolen outright would go back to its country of origin, art acquired through forced sales would likewise be returned, and German-owned art of uncertain status would sit in American trusteeship until Germany regained sovereignty. President Truman informally approved the plan on July 18, 1945. It was controversial from the start – a group of Monuments Men officers protested in the 1945 Wiesbaden Manifesto that some of the plan’s transfers amounted to the U.S. doing exactly what it had gone to war to stop – but it became the operating framework for American-zone restitution through the early 1950s, and President Eisenhower’s administration continued the process into the mid-1950s.51 Italy ran its own parallel campaign starting in 1946 under Rodolfo Siviero, a former intelligence officer turned art recovery specialist, who spent years tracking down Italian works moved into Germany.

The era’s clearest proof that restitution could actually work through the courts, not just occupation policy, came from neutral Switzerland. Paul Rosenberg, the Paris dealer who had represented Picasso, Matisse and Braque, returned to France in 1945 to find much of his collection gone, some of it having passed through Swiss dealers into private hands. He sued the dealer Theodor Fischer and the industrialist-collector Emil Bührle before the Swiss Federal Tribunal. On June 3, 1948, the court ordered the disputed works returned to Rosenberg and the defendants to cover his legal costs – an early and, for its time, unusually decisive judicial win for a claimant against private possessors in a country that had not been occupied and had no obligation to cooperate.52

The long silence, 1945–1990s

Alongside these individual cases, the postwar years built the institutional scaffolding that still underpins cultural-heritage law: UNESCO was founded in London in 1945; the Nuremberg trials prosecuted looting as a war crime; the International Council of Museums was created in Paris in 1946; and in 1954, the Hague Convention became the first treaty devoted entirely to protecting cultural property in armed conflict – though, true to the pattern set in 1899, it did not apply retroactively.

What that scaffolding did not do was address the largest single transfer of looted art in the postwar period, because for four decades almost nobody outside the Soviet Union was allowed to discuss it. Soviet “trophy brigades” removed an estimated 2.5 million artworks and artifacts from Germany and Eastern Europe as the Red Army advanced – some of it identifiable Nazi loot, much of it simply German cultural property treated as compensation for the staggering destruction the USSR had suffered. Rising Cold War tensions, especially around the 1948–49 Berlin Blockade, led Soviet authorities to cancel plans for a public trophy exhibition; instead, the material was quietly absorbed into state museum storage, unseen and largely unacknowledged for decades. Only after the Soviet Union’s collapse in 1991 did Russian officials begin to publicly confirm what they held.53

1995–1998: a convention, a conference, and a compromise

The modern restitution framework was built in a tight three-year window in the 1990s, and its central compromise still defines the field today. In June 1995, delegates in Rome adopted the UNIDROIT Convention, the first instrument to set binding private-law rules for returning stolen cultural property across borders: claims had to be filed within three years of the claimant learning where the object was and who held it, and in any case within fifty years of the theft.54 Russia signed and, for a time, said it intended to ratify; it never did, and by 1998 it had gone the opposite direction, passing a law that nationalized “trophy” art as Russian state property, permitted claims only from foreign governments rather than individuals, carved out broad exemptions for objects already held by museums or other institutions, and required Russia’s own parliament to approve any individual restitution by separate act – restitution made technically possible and practically almost never used.55

In the United States, the Association of Art Museum Directors issued a report in June 1998 urging its members to begin reviewing their own collections’ provenance for the Nazi era and to consult archives, dealers and scholars in doing so – a voluntary, good-faith standard, not a legal requirement, and one explicitly premised on the idea that “each claim presents a unique situation.” That same year, from November 30 to December 3, the U.S. State Department and the U.S. Holocaust Memorial Museum convened the Washington Conference on Holocaust-Era Assets, with Stuart Eizenstat and Abner Mikva co-chairing 57 delegations from 44 countries. The result, announced on the conference’s final day, was the Washington Principles on Nazi-Confiscated Art: a call to identify art that had been confiscated and never returned, fund the research needed to trace it, publish findings, and reach “just and fair” solutions with claimants.56

The Principles were deliberately not a treaty. Early in the negotiations there had been real appetite for something binding, but the gap between different countries’ legal systems, and the cost exposure a binding instrument would have created, made that unworkable; Switzerland proposed the compromise that ultimately let all 44 countries – including Russia – sign on to a shared statement of principle with no legal teeth.57 That trade-off is the single most important fact about the modern restitution regime: non-binding status is precisely what made near-universal agreement possible, and precisely why almost everything that followed has had the same structural weakness.

Uneven follow-through

What happened next varied enormously by country, largely because each government’s own legal architecture determined how much the Washington Principles could actually change.

Germany, which had the clearest moral and political stake, issued a Joint Declaration in 1999 committing to systematic research and restitution, a commitment that eventually produced the German Lost Art Foundation in 2015.58 Austria set up a provenance commission even before Washington, though its composition – dominated by political appointees rather than independent historians – drew criticism. France created the Commission pour l’Indemnisation des Victimes de Spoliations, but limited its reach to public collections, leaving private holdings essentially untouched.

Britain’s problem was structural: the British Museum Act of 1963 and similar legislation legally barred national museums from permanently transferring objects out of their collections at all, restitution claims included.59 A Spoliation Advisory Panel was established in 2000 to evaluate claims, but for its first nine years it had no legal mechanism to act on its own recommendations.60 The Holocaust (Return of Cultural Objects) Act 2009 finally gave listed institutions the power to return objects the Panel recommended, subject to government sign-off – but only for ten years, after which the power would lapse under a built-in sunset clause. Parliament ultimately voted to remove that expiration date in 2019, making the power permanent.61

The United States faced a different structural problem entirely: most major American museums are private institutions, which meant Washington had far less leverage over them than European governments had over their state-run counterparts. In practice, American restitution ran for years on voluntary self-policing – Sotheby’s opened the first restitution department at any major auction house in October 1997, a year ahead of the Washington Conference, and says its due-diligence work has since helped resolve claims on hundreds of artworks worth close to $1 billion combined62 – rather than legal compulsion. Lucian Simmons, the Sotheby’s vice president who oversaw that vetting process, described the standard behind it in 2016: “I think our moral compass has always remained the same, and that is that we want to protect our buyers and our sellers and not sell looted art which hasn’t been given back.”63 The compulsion came later and only partially: the Holocaust Expropriated Art Recovery Act, signed into law in December 2016, replaced a patchwork of state statutes of limitations with a single federal rule giving claimants six years from the point they actually discovered where a work was and who held it.64 Even that has proven an incomplete fix – museums and dealers have continued to lean on the separate doctrine of laches, which lets a court dismiss a claim simply for arriving “too late” regardless of when the claimant learned the facts – and the HEAR Act itself carried a sunset clause that required Congress to pass a fresh version in 2025 to keep the framework alive at all.65

What’s still missing

Every few years since 1998, the international community has essentially reconvened to say the same thing again, more emphatically. Thirty-eight countries endorsed “reasonable measures” to implement the Washington Principles at the Vilnius Forum in 2000. The Terezin Declaration in 2009 reaffirmed the same principles in stronger language, still without binding force. A 2009 UNESCO draft declaration covering the same ground introduced no new enforcement tools.66 On the twentieth anniversary of the Washington Principles in 2018, the United States and Germany issued a joint statement that went further than most in naming the problem directly, criticizing auction houses and dealers for continuing to exploit expired statutes of limitations and good-faith-buyer protections to keep contested works in circulation. In 2019, the European Parliament passed a resolution pushing for tighter rules – explicitly extending the same concern to art looted from active conflict zones in Syria, Iraq and Libya, a sign that the legal problem Rosenberg’s task force created in the 1940s has simply found new wars to attach itself to.

Where this leaves things

The Rosenberg v. Fischer and Bührle case is worth remembering, because it shows what restitution law looks like when it actually works: a known claimant, a known object, and a defendant sitting inside a court’s jurisdiction. Nearly every instrument built since 1945 – the Hague Convention, the Washington Principles, HEAR, the UK’s 2009 Act – is, in effect, an attempt to recreate those three conditions at larger scale, and each one has been undercut by the same recurring gaps: nothing is retroactive, almost nothing is binding, and claimants are consistently racing against a clock that started running long before they knew they had a claim to file.

That machinery was built for a specific kind of theft: state-sponsored, wartime, eventually followed by an occupying power with the authority to compel returns. It has essentially nothing to say about a painting that simply disappears through an ordinary private sale into an ownership structure nobody outside a small circle of dealers can see. A Van Gogh still life that sold at Sotheby’s in 2014 for $62 million, later traced by New York Times reporters to a jailed Chinese financier’s shell company, illustrates the gap precisely: it vanished not because it was stolen, but because it was bought. If a new wave of restitution law is coming, it will have to be built for that problem, not the one from 1945.

Chapter Four: Family Lines

Chen Dongsheng, Jen Hua, and the Political Bloodlines Behind Sotheby’s China Business

In November 2024, Claude Monet’s Nymphéas – a water-lilies canvas that had never before been offered publicly – sold at Sotheby’s New York for $65.5 million, the centerpiece of the late beauty-industry mogul Sydell Miller’s collection.67 The winning bid was placed by phone by Jen Hua, Sotheby’s Deputy Chairman of Asia and Chairman of China, on behalf of an unnamed client. Trade coverage of the sale – Artnet, ARTnews, Artsy, the usual outlets – described the price, the bidding contest, and Hua’s title.68 None of it said anything more about who Hua actually is.

Only one English-language source connects Jen Hua to her family: a May 30, 2023 post by Michael Forsythe, a New York Times investigative reporter: “Who is Jen Hua? Well, now she’s the head of Sotheby’s in China. But she is also the granddaughter of Hua Guofeng, the man who (briefly) replaced Chairman Mao as China’s leader.”69 Who Hua Guofeng actually was, what it meant to grow up as his granddaughter, and how that childhood produced the woman now closing Sotheby’s biggest deals in Asia exists almost entirely in Chinese-language sources: business profiles, entertainment coverage, and one substantial 2009 magazine interview in which Jen Hua spoke about her family for the first, and largely only, time.70

The man behind the name

Hua Guofeng was, for a few strange years, the most reproduced face in China. Mao Zedong hand-picked him as successor in 1976, reportedly on the strength of a six-character endorsement – “with you in charge, I am at ease” – that Hua treated for the rest of his political life as a mandate. He became Chairman of the Chinese Communist Party, premier, and chairman of the party’s Central Military Commission simultaneously, the only person in the PRC’s history to hold all three posts at once. What followed was a deliberate, state-organized cult of personality: Hua adopted Mao’s swept-back hairstyle, his mannerisms, his public cadence, and for a period the volume of official portraits and images of Hua actually exceeded those of Mao himself. It could not last. Deng Xiaoping, sidelined under Mao, maneuvered back into power through the late 1970s, and by 1981 Hua had been demoted to vice chairman before fading from active politics entirely.71

The China Hua Guofeng governed, however briefly, was still operating under the cultural doctrine Mao had laid down decades earlier at the 1942 Yan’an Forum on Literature and Art: that art belongs to a class and serves a political line, that it exists to reflect and elevate the working class rather than to explore individual or “bourgeois” sensibility.72 Western art movements built around subjective, individual expression were treated as symptoms of exactly the decadence the revolution existed to correct, a suspicion that hardened into open hostility during the Cultural Revolution, when Western-influenced art and literature were denounced and their practitioners persecuted. A granddaughter of the man who briefly inherited this entire ideological apparatus grew up to become the executive most responsible for selling Western Impressionist and Modern paintings into wealthy Chinese hands.

Hua Guofeng and his wife, Han Zhijun, had four children, and the family carried the surname Su rather than Hua: “Hua Guofeng” was itself a revolutionary-era adopted name, and his children kept the family’s original surname: Su Hua, the eldest son, who built an entire Air Force career, rising to general, without colleagues knowing whose son he was until after he retired; Su Bin, the second son, who moved into real estate and equity investment, including a stint co-chairing a coal-investment fund and a board seat at a Hong Kong-listed energy company; and two daughters, Su Ling and Su Li, the younger of whom served for years as her father’s personal secretary.73 Chinese entertainment coverage identifies Jen Hua’s own father as Su Bin – the businessman son, not the general.74 Hua Guofeng spent his later years tending a vineyard, distributing the grapes among his staff, and watching his four granddaughters grow up in the same courtyard over roughly two decades: Su Yan, Hua Jiezhen, Wang Sujia, and Guo Qing. Of the four, only Hua Jiezhen – Jen Hua – kept the Hua name professionally, a detail Chinese profiles consistently read as a sign of particular closeness between grandfather and granddaughter; by multiple accounts, the name was chosen by Hua Guofeng himself.75 Her birth year is given as 1981 in one of the profiles consulted; it could not be independently cross-confirmed.76

Growing up unnamed

Jen Hua’s own childhood is where Chinese-language accounts genuinely diverge. The original user draft this piece is built on places her at a school in Maizidian, a then-rural stretch of Beijing’s Chaoyang district, boarding on weekdays and going home only on weekends. Several Chinese profiles instead center the story on a village called Zaoying, also in the Chaoyang area: one describes it simply as the public school her mother chose over an elite alternative, while two others describe an extended placement there – one specifying six years, during elementary and middle school – doing agricultural work rather than attending an urban school.77 One of those profiles attributes to her the line: “I feel life is like an unpredictable auction; some things appear cheap but are priceless. My days at Zaoying, though materially poor, brought invaluable experience.”78 Across every version, the point is the same: she was deliberately kept away from the privilege her grandfather’s name could have bought her, and instructed never to mention him to teachers or classmates.

She later left for the United States – Indiana University Bloomington, per her official Sotheby’s biography, where she earned bachelor’s degrees in Communication and Culture and in East Asian Language and Studies, with a minor in Marketing79 – where, by her own later account, nobody had any idea who her family was, and where she earned those degrees while working, according to one Chinese profile, as a restaurant server and library assistant to help cover her own costs.80 She returned to China for an executive MBA at Tsinghua University, confirmed by the same official biography (Chinese profiles describe the same degree less precisely, as a “Master’s in Business Administration” or simply “an MBA”)81, and a graduate diamonds diploma from the Gemological Institute of America, the credential every account agrees got her into Sotheby’s in the first place.82

Her marriage is one point on which the sources flatly contradict each other. She was married to Li Yundi, the concert pianist who in 2000 became the first Chinese musician to win the International Chopin Piano Competition; every profile agrees on that much. One dates the marriage’s end to November 2012. Another dates its start to 2013. Both cannot be correct, and nothing in the sources consulted resolves which one is.83

She broke her public silence about her grandfather in 2009, at 28, in a rare interview with Bazaar Jewelry – the first time she spoke publicly about him at all. The original research behind this piece renders her words as a description of the discomfort of being “that little girl at Grandfather’s side.” A separate Chinese profile attributes to her a related but distinct line: “At first I didn’t like being called Hua Guofeng’s granddaughter, but growing up I gradually understood: grandfather was an extraordinarily great man, and I’m very proud to be his granddaughter.”84 Both versions describe the same arc, wanting to be known for what she herself had built rather than her lineage. What she built was a career: joining a Sotheby’s operation in Asia that traces back roughly fifty years, to the house’s first Hong Kong sales in the early 1970s, and rising from Deputy Chairman of China (2016–2019) to her current post, Deputy Chairman of Asia and Chairman of China, a title Sotheby’s announced in early 202385 – the job that had her on the phone in November 2024, closing a $65.5 million Monet. One profile also credits her with founding a charitable foundation supporting disadvantaged children, a detail not corroborated elsewhere.86

A parallel line, through marriage rather than blood

Jen Hua is not the only person at Sotheby’s whose family runs directly into the top of the Chinese Communist Party – she is simply the one whose connection runs through blood and an executive title, rather than marriage and an ownership stake.

Chen Dongsheng, born in 1957 in Jingling, Tianmen, in Hubei province, graduated from Wuhan University’s economics department in 1983 and later earned a doctorate in economics. He founded China Guardian, one of China’s first major auction houses, in 1993, and founded and has chaired Taikang – now one of China’s largest insurers, with assets the Financial Times put at roughly RMB 33 billion, or $6 billion, at the time of the Sotheby’s deal – since 1996.87 He is married to Kong Dongmei, a granddaughter of Mao Zedong through Mao’s daughter Li Min.88

Over June and July 2016, Chen’s Taikang Life bought 7.918 million Sotheby’s shares on the open market for $232.7 million, a 13.5 percent stake that made it the auction house’s single largest shareholder. Sotheby’s CEO Tad Smith said at the time that the board had met with Taikang’s executives and would “warmly welcome their support of Sotheby’s strategic initiatives”89 – a statement of corporate diplomacy that gave no hint of whose granddaughter-in-law’s money had just bought its way onto the board’s radar. The purchase was reported at the time across Western financial and trade press purely as a corporate transaction, with Chen’s marriage into Mao’s family treated, where it was mentioned at all, as a footnote rather than the point.

Chen surfaces once more in the public record in a context that has nothing to do with Sotheby’s directly: the 2016 Panama Papers leak. Records from the leaked Mossack Fonseca archive show that in 2011 Chen used the firm to establish Keen Best International Limited, a British Virgin Islands company of which he was sole director and shareholder; what the entity was actually used for is not established in the leaked files, and there is no public suggestion of wrongdoing attached to it. Chen was far from alone – Chinese clients accounted for roughly 29 percent of Mossack Fonseca’s business, by some distance the firm’s most lucrative single market, and the leak named a wide swath of relatives of senior Chinese officials as operators of offshore vehicles.90

By Chen’s own account, his fascination with the Western auction world traces to a single moment in 1987: watching, on television, Vincent van Gogh’s Sunflowers sell for $39.9 million in London – the purchase, by Yasuda Fire and Marine Insurance, that set off a buying spree among Japanese corporations at the height of the country’s 1980s asset bubble. Chen has described the scene as feeling impossibly distant from his own life in what was then, in his words, an economically backward country. Before founding China Guardian in 1993, he attended a Sotheby’s sale in Hong Kong with a video camera his brother had bought him in Japan and secretly filmed the auction room from the back row, studying the footage so he could later replicate how a Western house actually ran a sale.91 Within three decades he had gone from filming Sotheby’s from the audience to owning the largest single stake in the company.

One account goes further, describing Chen’s stake as something closer to a symbol: China’s soft-power ambitions in the art world, made literal by a shareholding.92

The buyout that ended one line, not the other

Chen’s ownership stake did not survive Sotheby’s next transformation. When French-Israeli telecom billionaire Patrick Drahi acquired Sotheby’s for $3.7 billion in 2019 and took the 275-year-old house private, the deal forced a sale of every outstanding share, Chen’s included – ending his ownership position, though not before he had profited substantially from the stock’s run-up ahead of the sale.93

Jen Hua’s promotion to Deputy Chairman of Asia and Chairman of China came in January 2023, under Drahi’s private ownership, four years after Chen’s exit. Her family’s connection to Hua Guofeng, like Chen’s marriage into Mao’s family before it, has never been reported in English beyond Forsythe’s single tweet.

Chapter Five: The Leverage

What a $3.7 Billion Bet on Independence Actually Bought

In June 2019, after 31 years trading on the New York Stock Exchange under the ticker BID, Sotheby’s went private. Patrick Drahi, the French-Israeli telecommunications billionaire behind Altice, paid $3.7 billion through his holding company BidFair USA, funded in part through French bank BNP Paribas and the sale of up to $400 million of Drahi’s own stake in Altice USA.94 The pitch to shareholders, and to the art world, was straightforward: freed from quarterly earnings calls and activist investors, the 275-year-old auction house could finally move as fast as its fastest-growing market required. That market was, unmistakably, Asia.

Six years on, the record is split cleanly in two. At home, the leverage that bought Sotheby’s its independence has left it laying off staff, delaying payments, and answering for a corruption scandal inside its owner’s telecom empire. In Asia, the freedom that leverage was supposed to buy appears to be working roughly as advertised. Both halves of that record are real, and neither cancels the other out.

Who was there before

For most of its modern life, Sotheby’s was the cautious half of a two-house duopoly. Christie’s, its only real rival, had been privately owned since 1998 by François Pinault’s Artemis holding company, free to offer sellers aggressive guarantees – a promise to pay a minimum price regardless of what the work fetches at auction – without having to explain the resulting risk to public shareholders every quarter. Sotheby’s, listed on the New York Stock Exchange, had no such freedom: guarantees show up as liabilities on a public company’s books, and Wall Street did not love a 275-year-old auction house taking on private-equity-style risk. Sotheby’s competed anyway, more conservatively, and periodically lost major consignments to Christie’s specifically because it could not match a guarantee its own board considered too exposed to disclose.

That restraint had a name and a face: William Ruprecht, Sotheby’s CEO since 2000 (and a company veteran for two decades before that), a career auctioneer who ran the house the way it had always been run – steadily, deliberately, allergic to the kind of balance-sheet risk that shows up badly in a shareholder letter.95 To a growing chorus of investors, that steadiness looked like drift, and it became the explicit target of an activist campaign that began in August 2013, when Daniel Loeb’s Third Point LLC started quietly buying Sotheby’s stock. By October, Third Point held a 9.4 percent stake and Loeb went public, comparing Sotheby’s to “an old master painting in need of restoration” and demanding Ruprecht’s resignation outright. Loeb wanted the company leaner and more aggressive: sell its Manhattan headquarters, tighten the balance sheet, and stop ceding consignments to a more nimble Christie’s.96 Sotheby’s management dug in, spending roughly $20 million to defend itself and its CEO, before the two sides settled in May 2014: Third Point got three board seats and $10 million in reimbursed campaign costs.97 It proved the beginning of the end for Ruprecht rather than a final defeat – with activist-aligned directors now inside the boardroom, he stepped down later that year, and the directors who had pushed him out were, five years later, the same class of shareholder ready to back a sale to the next outsider who promised to finish the job.98 Two years after Ruprecht’s departure, in July 2016, Chen Dongsheng’s Taikang Life bought a 13.5 percent stake for $230 million, making it Sotheby’s single largest shareholder.

Ruprecht-era restraint was not purely a matter of temperament, either – in China specifically, it was a matter of law. Sotheby’s could only enter the mainland at all through a joint venture with a state-owned partner, Beijing Gehua Art Company – a structure that gave Sotheby’s operational control but also tied it to a Chinese state entity in a way regulators, and cautious Chinese buyers, could not simply overlook. Christie’s, arriving a year later as a fully independent, wholly foreign-owned enterprise with no state joint-venture partner, could operate under its own name without that entanglement, even as it faced its own restriction on trading cultural relics predating 1911. Sotheby’s, in other words, wanted precisely what Christie’s had – unencumbered access to mainland buyers and sellers – and Chinese law, not just Wall Street, was part of what stood in the way for years.

Patrick Drahi, when he appeared in 2019, was a genuine outsider to that fight: a telecommunications billionaire largely unknown in the art world, though reportedly a longtime, reclusive private client of the major houses. What he offered was, in effect, the thing Loeb’s campaign had been agitating for all along – freedom from exactly the public-market caution that had cost Sotheby’s consignments to Christie’s for years. Going private meant Sotheby’s could match or beat a rival’s guarantee without disclosing the exposure to shareholders every quarter, and could pursue private sales – deals brokered quietly between a seller and a buyer, with no public auction, no public price, and no public scrutiny – as aggressively as it wanted. Loeb, whose board seats gave him a direct vote, backed the deal; shareholders approved it by roughly 91 percent, taking a 61 percent premium on their shares in the process. Sotheby’s board chairman put the rationale plainly at the time: after more than three decades as a public company, “the time is right for Sotheby’s to return to private ownership to continue on a path of growth and success.”99

The debt

A leveraged buyout is, structurally, a bet that a company’s future profits will cover the debt taken on to acquire it. By mid-2024, Sotheby’s total debt stood at roughly $1.8 billion, against a debt-to-earnings ratio of about ten times – a heavy load for a business whose revenue depends on collectors deciding, discretionarily, to sell. That bet has not been going well. A leaked internal report put Sotheby’s core earnings down 88 percent and its auction sales down 25 percent in the first half of 2024 alone, against a global art market that was itself contracting.100

The strain showed up first in the back office. Sotheby’s gave staff IOUs in place of bonuses and delayed payments to contracted workers, including the shippers who move the art itself. Layoffs followed in waves: at least ten senior specialists let go in the summer of 2023, roughly 50 staff cut from the London office in May 2024, and a further 100 positions eliminated in New York later that year.101

A costly detour into crypto

Sotheby’s leaned hard into one growth bet that made the underlying strain worse rather than better. In October 2021, at the peak of the NFT boom, the house launched “Sotheby’s Metaverse,” a dedicated platform for digital-collectible sales, and moved roughly $100 million of NFT art in 2021 alone – including an $11.8 million CryptoPunk that June and a $3.4 million Bored Ape that October.102 The timing could hardly have been worse. NFT trading volume collapsed through 2022, and Sotheby’s own credibility took a public hit when the anonymous owner of a bundle of 104 CryptoPunks pulled the lot from a scheduled sale at the last minute and boasted online about having “rugged” the house.103 By 2024, Sotheby’s was auctioning individual CryptoPunks – the same category that had fetched eight figures three years earlier – for as little as $66,000.104

Scandal in the parent company

The financial strain compounded with a personal one. In July 2023, Portuguese authorities arrested Armando Pereira, Drahi’s co-founder at Altice and one of his closest longtime partners, and placed him under house arrest as part of “Operation Picoas,” an investigation into corruption, fiscal fraud and money laundering. Prosecutors allege Pereira rigged Altice’s supplier contracts across roughly 60 companies, diverting several hundred million euros. Drahi’s own response, delivered to investors, was unusually personal: “This was a shock and a big disappointment for me. If the suspicions of the Portuguese tax authorities turn out to be true, I will feel betrayed.” French prosecutors opened their own preliminary corruption probe tied to the same network in 2024.105

A second Altice executive was drawn into the same investigation days later. Tatiana Agova-Bregou, Altice France’s director of content, acquisitions and partnerships, was suspended in August 2023; Portuguese prosecutors suspect she benefited from a roughly €1.7 million apartment in Neuilly-sur-Seine, outside Paris, purchased on her behalf by an associate of Pereira’s. Her lawyers say she committed no wrongdoing and was surprised to be linked to the case at all.106

Separate from the Altice scandal, Drahi has faced his own tax dispute – in Switzerland, not, as sometimes assumed, in France. Swiss reporting alleges that in 2005 Drahi and his wife announced a separation to tax authorities in order to qualify him for Geneva-area lump-sum taxation calculated on lifestyle rather than income; Geneva’s tax authority is now investigating the 2009–2016 period and, according to that reporting, seeking billions of euros in back taxes and penalties. Drahi’s representatives have declined to comment.107 Whatever the outcome, the financial toll of these years is visible in the numbers Forbes tracks regardless of any single dispute: Drahi’s net worth stood at roughly $8.4 billion in May 2019, around the time of the Sotheby’s deal, and had fallen to about $4.9 billion by the end of 2023.108

The IPO that wasn’t

For a moment in 2021, it looked as though Sotheby’s independence might be short-lived by design. Riding a record $7.3 billion sales year, the house engaged Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley to explore a U.S. listing that could have valued the business at roughly $5 billion excluding debt – an IPO just over two years after Drahi had taken it private, which would have let him cash out a substantial position while retaining control.109 The plans went quiet through 2022 and 2023, and by December 2023 Sotheby’s had reportedly abandoned the listing altogether; CEO Charles Stewart told Bloomberg the business had no need to raise capital and denied Drahi was pursuing a public offering.110

The bet that’s working

Set against all of that is the part of the strategy Drahi’s team has actually executed with some discipline: building out Asia.

In 2021, Drahi installed his own son, Nathan – then 26, previously at J.P. Morgan and the private-equity firm BC Partners, and only a year into his Sotheby’s career – as managing director of the Asia business, succeeding Kevin Ching, who had run the region for 15 years.111 The appointment drew the obvious skepticism a founder’s-son promotion invites. What followed was a genuine buildout: a new 2,000-square-meter space in Shanghai opened in May 2023 as Sotheby’s marked its fiftieth anniversary in Asia,112 followed in 2024 by a flagship “Maison” in the Landmark Chater complex at the center of Hong Kong’s luxury retail district and a new 36,000-square-foot regional headquarters at Six Pacific Place.113 Sotheby’s Asia sales reached $1.1 billion in 2023, matching the region’s all-time high set in 2021; roughly a third of the house’s Asia bidders were entirely new to Sotheby’s, and Asian collectors were bidding roughly 40 percent more per person, on average, than collectors elsewhere.114 Nathan Drahi himself relocated to New York in February 2025 after five years building the Asia operation, handing the region to a new managing director, Masumi Shinohara115 – a transition that reads less like a retreat than like a business mature enough to no longer need the founder’s son running it directly.

Two sales from that stretch illustrate what the buildout was chasing. On April 5, 2023, Zhang Daqian’s splashed-color screen Hua Kai Shi Zhang Ying Cenci (“Flowers Blooming Ten Feet Tall, Shadows Uneven”), painted in 1973 and marketed in English as Pink Lotuses on Gold Screen, opened at HK$150 million, hammered after fifteen minutes of bidding at HK$216.5 million, and sold with premium for HK$251,648,500 (about $32 million) – the year’s top result for modern and contemporary Chinese painting and calligraphy, the third-highest price ever paid for the artist, and a record for any Zhang Daqian lotus painting specifically.116,117 Three days later, an 18th-century Qianlong-era Falangcai porcelain bowl decorated with apricot trees and swallows, from the collection of Dr. Alice Cheng (Zhang Yongzhen), sold for HK$198,220,000 (about $25.5 million) – the same bowl that had set a Chinese-art auction record when Cheng bought it in 2006 for HK$151.32 million, a seventeen-year hold that outperformed most conventional assets over the same period.118,119 China, whose art market had contracted sharply the year before, grew 9 percent in 2023 and reclaimed its position as the world’s second-largest art market behind the United States.

What the leverage bought

Put the two halves of the record side by side and the shape of the bet becomes clear. The debt that freed Sotheby’s from Wall Street’s quarterly scrutiny is the same debt now weighing on a balance sheet that delayed paychecks and cut a hundred jobs in New York in a single year. The corporate freedom that let Drahi install his own son to run the Asia business, and the corporate opacity that shielded his telecom empire from the scrutiny that might have caught its corruption sooner, are not cause and effect. They are the same fact, applied twice: an ownership structure built to answer to Drahi alone. And the market that the whole leveraged bet was ultimately aimed at is generating $1.1 billion a year and has reclaimed its rank as the world’s second-largest: proof, on the numbers Drahi’s team can actually point to, that the freedom the debt bought was not wasted, whatever it cost everywhere else.

Chapter Six: The Collectors Who Bought Like It Was Free

Liu Yiqian, Wang Wei, and the Long Museum

In April 2014, at Sotheby’s Hong Kong, a Shanghai investor named Liu Yiqian paid $36.3 million for a Ming Dynasty teacup. The “Chicken Cup,” a 15th-century vessel just over three inches wide and decorated with a hen tending her chicks, set a record for the highest price ever paid for Chinese porcelain at auction – one of only seventeen such cups known to survive, four of them in private hands. Liu paid with an American Express card, reportedly racking up millions of reward points in the process. Then, in a photograph that circulated across the art world for weeks, he drank tea directly out of it.120

The stunt captured something real about Liu Yiqian, and about the collecting era he came to symbolize: a former taxi driver and middle-school dropout who built a fortune in financial services and then spent it on trophies with a swagger that scandalized more traditional collectors.121 It also obscures something else – that the more consequential half of his art-world story belongs to his wife, Wang Wei, whose own collecting eventually produced one of the most influential private museums in China, built on a completely different premise from her husband’s headline-grabbing purchases.

From cabs to catalogs

Liu’s entry into the art world traces to a specific, well-documented afternoon in Beijing in 1993. He had traveled from Shanghai to buy internal employee shares in a state enterprise and happened upon China Guardian’s inaugural auction. He paid 200 RMB for a catalog and recognized only two names in it, Li Keran and Guo Moruo, so he spent 180,000 RMB on Li Keran’s ink lotus painting and a Guo Moruo calligraphy of a Mao Zedong poem – a modest start, by his later standards, for a collector who would go on to define what Chinese dealers began calling the market’s “billion-yuan era.”122,123 By the time of the Chicken Cup purchase two decades later, Forbes estimated his fortune at roughly $900 million, built through Sunline Group, a financial-services conglomerate with holdings in securities, real estate and pharmaceuticals.124

What set Liu apart from more established collectors wasn’t just the size of his checks but his stated indifference to the connoisseurship norms those collectors valued. He gravitated toward the single most expensive, most historically loaded object in a sale rather than building a scholarly collection around a period or theme, and he said so openly: in interviews, he and Wang Wei have both described spending more than half of their household income on art, “because we love art”125 – a framing that treats acquisition itself, not resale or connoisseurship, as the point.

The trophies

The Chicken Cup was the loudest of Liu’s purchases, but not the only one built for headlines. In November 2014, at Christie’s Hong Kong, he paid HK$348.44 million – roughly $45 million – for a 600-year-old embroidered silk thangka commissioned during the reign of the Yongle Emperor, depicting the Buddhist deity Raktayamari. The work had left China roughly two centuries earlier; Liu described the purchase, at the time a record for a Chinese work of art sold through an international auction house, as bringing it home.126 A year later, at Christie’s New York, he and Wang Wei paid $170.4 million for Amedeo Modigliani’s Nu Couché, a purchase that, notably, went straight into public display at their museum rather than a private vault.

Wang Wei’s turn

Liu’s wife built a parallel, and ultimately more institutionally significant, collection of her own. Where Liu chased marquee individual objects, Wang Wei developed a sustained interest in Chinese oil paintings from the revolutionary period – work made from the Communist base at Yan’an in the 1930s and ’40s through the socialist decades that followed, much of it by artists trained in France, the Soviet Union, and Japan during China’s early Republican era. By 2009 she had assembled enough of this material, which she called her “Red Classics” collection, to mount a full exhibition: “Revolutionary Art,” curated with Chen Lüsheng, then vice director of the National Museum of China, bringing together more than 200 works spanning from the Yan’an era to the present and featuring artists including Jin Shangyi, Chen Yanning and Chen Yifei.127

That exhibition became the template for something larger. With Liu’s financial backing, the couple opened Long Museum Pudong in 2012, followed in March 2014 by Long Museum West Bund – together forming what the couple describes as a “One City, Two Museums” system, and what is generally regarded as China’s first major privately founded art museum network.128

One City, Two Museums

The West Bund building, designed by the Chinese architect Liu Yichun of Atelier Deshaus rather than one of the international starchitecture firms more commonly hired for prestige museum commissions in China, covers 33,000 square meters, with 16,000 reserved for exhibition space. Its distinctive umbrella-vaulted concrete construction incorporates the remnants of the site’s earlier industrial life – a 1950s coal-conveying platform and a more recent parking structure – into the finished design, on the architect’s stated theory that “a ruin gives a sense of freedom and is where the energy of nature hides.” The galleries borrow their daylighting approach from Louis Kahn.129 Wang Wei has described the museum’s mission in blunt terms: “Museum education is even more important than what we learn from textbooks.”130

A test of the philosophy

For over a decade, the Long Museum operated as a one-way accumulation engine: Liu and Wang bought, and almost nothing left the collection. That changed in the fall of 2023, when the couple consigned roughly 40 works – spanning René Magritte, Léonard Foujita, Zao Wou-Ki, David Hockney and Modigliani – to Sotheby’s Hong Kong for a sale titled “A Long Journey: A Selection from the Liu Yiqian and Wang Wei Collection,” held September 22 through October 5, 2023. Sotheby’s estimated the group would bring between HK$745 million and HK$1.6 billion, roughly $95 million to $135 million.131

It did not. The sale totaled HK$544.5 million, about $69.5 million – less than three-quarters of even the low estimate, and by most trade-press accounts a genuine disappointment rather than a strategic soft landing.132 The lead lot, Modigliani’s 1919 portrait Paulette Jourdain, carried an on-request estimate around HK$350 million (roughly $45 million); the couple had bought it at Sotheby’s New York in November 2015, from the estate sale of Sotheby’s own former chairman A. Alfred Taubman, for $42.8 million.133 It sold on October 5, 2023, for a hammer price of HK$235 million – a final price with fees of about $34.8 million, roughly 20 percent below what the couple had paid for it eight years earlier.134 That is a real loss, on the order of $8 million – notably smaller than the far larger figure sometimes attached to this sale in secondhand retellings, which appear to have confused the picture’s total sale price with the size of the loss itself.

Commentary on the sale split along familiar lines: some attributed the shortfall to a broadly cooling market for exactly the kind of blue-chip Western modernism the couple had built their reputation on, others to presale estimates that had simply been set too high for a single-owner sale this large.135 Both explanations can be true at once, and neither requires assuming, as some speculation at the time did, that the consignment reflected financial distress at the Long Museum – the couple’s own public framing was that the sale represented a small fraction of a much larger collection, consigned to fund future acquisitions and museum programming rather than to raise emergency cash.

What the sale actually tested

Whatever the cause, the result mattered less for its size than for what it demonstrated: that Liu Yiqian’s founding premise – buy the best object in the room and worry about the market later – had never actually been tested against a down market until 2023, because he had spent three decades collecting almost entirely through a period of relentless upward pricing. The Chicken Cup, the thangka, the Modigliani nude that stayed on public view rather than disappearing into a vault: all of it was bought during the same multi-decade run of Asian capital entering the Western and Chinese art markets, a run that began with Japan’s 1980s bubble and continued through the mainland’s own rise. The 2023 sale was the first moment that run’s other direction – the possibility of loss – showed up in Liu and Wang’s own numbers, on a single afternoon in Hong Kong, in public.
